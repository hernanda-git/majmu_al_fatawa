The Seventh Principle:

It may be stated as follows: Much of what “revelation” establishes can also be known “through reason.” The Quran explains the evidence upon which reason relies, directs people to it, and draws attention to it, as Allah has mentioned in numerous places.

For He—Glorified and Exalted is He—has explained the signs that point to Him, His oneness, His power, His knowledge, and other matters, guiding His servants to these signs and directing them to them. He has likewise explained the evidence establishing the prophethood of His prophets, as well as the evidence establishing the resurrection and its possibility.

These matters are grounded in revelation in two respects:
- The Lawgiver has informed us of them, and He has explained the rational evidence by which they are established.

The examples presented in the Quran are “rational arguments”; this has been discussed at length elsewhere. These matters are also rational, since they can be known through reason as well. Many practitioners of speculative theology call these “rational principles,” because they believe that they

## Page 967

(ب)
can be known only through reason. For revelation consists merely in the report of a truthful speaker, and the report of that truthful speaker—the Prophet—cannot be known to be true until these principles have been established through reason. They may then disagree over the principles upon which establishing prophethood depends.

“One group” claims that reason’s determination of moral good and evil is among these principles, and that prophethood cannot be established without it. They regard denial of divine decree as something that reason rules out.

“Another group” claims that the world’s coming into existence is among these principles, and that knowledge of the Creator is impossible without establishing that the world came into existence. Establishing this, in turn, is impossible without establishing that bodies came into existence. Their coming into existence is known either through the coming into existence of their attributes or through that of the acts inhering in them. They therefore make the denial of the Lord’s acts and attributes principles without which prophethood cannot be established.

These people then refuse to accept arguments from the Quran and Sunnah against their position, supposing that reason conflicts with revelation—while being its foundation—and must therefore take precedence over it. Revelation must consequently either be reinterpreted or left to Allah. On closer examination, they also refuse to accept arguments from the Quran and Sunnah supporting their position, for the reasons already mentioned. These people go astray in several respects:

---

## Page 968

(ج)

One of these is their supposition that revelation is sometimes conveyed through reports. This is not the case. Rather, the Quran sets forth rational proofs by which religious truths are known, the like of which cannot be found in the writings of the leading thinkers engaged in rational inquiry. These truths are thus established both by revelation and by reason.

Another is their supposition that the Messenger’s truthfulness can be known only through the particular method they have followed. They are certainly mistaken in limiting the ways of establishing his truthfulness to what they have mentioned. There are many ways of knowing that the Messenger is truthful, as has been explained at length elsewhere.

Another is their supposition that the method they have followed is sound, when it may be invalid.

Another is their supposition that what they invoke against revelation is known through reason. They are mistaken in this: when weighed on the correct scale, what contradicts the Quran and Sunnah proves to belong among things unknown, not among truths established by reason. This has been discussed at length elsewhere.

The point here is that some of “the attributes of Allah Most High” may be known through reason, just as it is known that He possesses knowledge, power, and life. His words direct us to this:

“Does He who created not know?”

Those engaged in rational inquiry who affirm the divine attributes agree that, according to the discerning scholars, it is known through reason that He possesses life, knowledge, power, and will. Likewise, hearing, sight, and speech are established

---

## Page 969

(د)
through reason, according to the discerning scholars. Indeed, love, pleasure, and anger can likewise be established through reason. His being above created things and separate from them is also known through reason, as the leading scholars have established on this basis, including Ahmad ibn Hanbal and others, as well as ‘Abd al-Ālī al-Makkī and ‘Abd Allāh ibn Sa‘īd ibn Kullāb.

Indeed, the possibility of seeing Him can likewise be established through reason. Some have established it on the grounds that anything that exists can be seen; others have established it on the grounds that anything self-subsisting can be seen. The latter approach is sounder than the former. It may also be possible to establish the possibility of seeing Him by an approach other than these two, through a division between affirmation and negation, as when one says: seeing depends only on factors that exist, and whatever depends only on factors that exist is more properly applicable to the necessary, eternal Being than to a contingent being brought into existence.

These matters are discussed at length elsewhere. The point here is that one of the approaches followed in this matter by the leading scholars and the Sunni thinkers who follow them is that, if He did not possess one of two opposing attributes, He would necessarily possess the other. Thus, if He were not described as possessing life, He would be described as possessing death; and if He were not described

---

## Page 970

(هـ)
as possessing power, He would be described as possessing incapacity; and if He were not described as possessing hearing, sight, and speech, He would be described as possessing deafness, muteness, and speechlessness. Applying the same reasoning, if He were not described as separate from the world, He would be within it. Thus, negating one of two opposing attributes of Him entails affirming the other. That other attribute is a deficiency from which even a perfect created being is free; the Creator is therefore all the more deserving of being declared free from it.

This approach differs from our saying that these are attributes of perfection possessed by created beings, and that the Creator is therefore all the more deserving of them. Establishing attributes of perfection in their own right differs from establishing them by negating what contradicts them. A group of those who deny the divine attributes raised a well-known objection to this approach, misleading people with it. Consequently, many who affirm the attributes came to regard the objection as sound and, on its basis, to consider the argument for affirmation weak. Some speculative thinkers did this, even al-Āmidī and others like him (1), although this objection forms the basis of the position held by the esoteric Qarmatians and their like among the Jahmiyyah.

They said:

The assertion that, if He did not possess these attributes—such as hearing, sight, and speech—while being alive, He would possess their opposites can only be properly examined once the nature of two opposites and their categories have been explained. We therefore say:

(و)
Two opposites cannot coexist in the same thing in the same respect. Either they cannot both be true or both be false, or they can coincide in one of these two ways. For they are opposed by negation and affirmation, which is the opposition of contradiction. Contradiction is the difference between two propositions in negation and affirmation such that, by their very nature, they cannot both be true or both be false. For example:

“Zayd is an animal”; “Zayd is not an animal.”

A distinctive consequence of the impossibility of both alternatives being true or both being false is that there is no intermediate between them, nor does either alternative undergo transformation in the same respect. They cannot both be true or both be false. For an existent being’s being intrinsically necessary and its being intrinsically contingent can neither coexist nor both be absent.

Thus, if you adopt this classification, defining the two contradictories as follows:

“Two contradictories can neither coexist nor both be absent,”

then these two can neither coexist nor both be absent, yet they are not negation and affirmation. It is therefore incorrect to restrict contradictories—which can neither coexist nor both be absent—to negation and affirmation. Two attributes—two things—have thus been established that can neither coexist nor both be absent, yet this falls outside the four categories.

Accordingly, someone who regards death as a positive existent property may say that a thing’s necessarily possessing either life or death belongs to this category. The same applies to knowledge and ignorance, deafness, muteness, and similar attributes.

(The second argument)

It may be said: This classification contains overlapping categories. Privation and possession fall under negation and affirmation and are, at most, a subtype of that opposition. Correlatives likewise fall under contraries and are merely a subtype of them.

If he says, “By negation and affirmation I mean what does not fall under privation and possession—namely, denying of a thing what it is incapable of possessing. This is why one of its distinctive properties is held to be that neither of its two terms undergoes transformation,” and so forth, he may be answered in two ways.

The first is that this amounts only to dividing negation into two kinds: first, denying what a thing can possess; and second, denying what it cannot possess.

It may then be said: The first is the affirmation of what a thing can, but need not, possess; the second is the affirmation of what it must possess. Thus, what is meant is the negation of what is impossible and the affirmation of what is necessary. For example, “Zayd is an animal” is a necessary affirmation, while “Zayd is not a stone” negates what is impossible.

On this understanding, contingent things capable of existence and nonexistence—as when we say, “A triangle is either existent or nonexistent”—would fall under the category of privation and possession. Yet this is not so. For

---

## Page 973

(ح)
in that category, a single subject can lack both opposites, whereas no contingent thing can be neither existent nor nonexistent. Moreover, on this understanding, all the Lord’s attributes are necessary to Him. Thus, saying, “Either He is living, or knowing, or hearing, or seeing, or speaking—or He is not,” is like saying, “Either He exists or He does not.” The latter is an opposition of negation and affirmation, so the former must be likewise. This establishes the point intended.

If it is said, “This is not valid unless it is known that He can possess these attributes,” the reply is: This overlap between the two occurs only with attributes that a subject can possess and then lose, as in the case of an animal. As for the Lord, Most High, if these attributes are affirmed of Him, they are necessarily His. For all rational people agree that He cannot alternate between possessing them and lacking them. That would entail His being alive at one time and dead at another, deaf at one time and hearing at another. This would entail His being characterized by imperfections, which is categorically ruled out.

The case is different with someone who denies these attributes and says that denying them implies no imperfection, because he supposes that He cannot possess them. Someone who says this cannot maintain that, even if He could possess them, denying them would imply no imperfection. The invalidity of this is self-evident.

He may also be told: If, in the opposition of negation and affirmation, you require that both alternatives be known to be possible, you could not say that the Necessary Existent is either existent or nonexistent;

---

## Page 974

(ط)
or that what cannot exist is either existent or nonexistent; for here one alternative is known to obtain, while the other is known to be impossible. If, however, you require knowledge that one of the alternatives is possible, it is valid to say: either He is living or He is not; either He is hearing and seeing or He is not. For if the negation is possible, the division is valid; if it is impossible, the affirmation is necessary, and the intended point is established.

If it is said, “This shows that this interpretation falls under the opposition of negation and affirmation, and we concede that, as stated in the objection. But all it establishes is that He is either hearing or not hearing, and either seeing or not seeing; the opponent chooses the negation,” he may be told: On this assumption, what is affirmed is necessary, and what is denied is impossible. Thus, these attributes are either necessary to Him or impossible for Him. There is no basis for claiming that they are impossible, since there is no evidence whatsoever for that claim.

Indeed, it may be said: We know self-evidently that the claim of impossibility is false. For their impossibility can only be argued on the same grounds used to deny the attributes altogether, and the invalidity of those grounds is already known. It must therefore be affirmed that these attributes are necessary to Him.

Know that this can serve as an independent method of affirming His attributes of perfection. They are either necessary to Him or impossible for Him. The latter is false, so the former must be true; for His being capable

of bearing them while lacking them would entail His being contingent, which is impossible in His case. This is a method familiar to those speculative thinkers who have followed it.

(The second response is to say:
On this account, if we say, “Zayd is either rational or not rational; either knowledgeable or not knowledgeable; either living or not living; either speaking or not speaking,” or make similar statements that negate an attribute of a subject capable of bearing it, this would not fall under the opposition of negation and affirmation. Yet this plainly contradicts what is self-evidently known, the consensus of rational people, and what they themselves have stated in logic and elsewhere. It is well known that such propositions contradict one another through negation and affirmation: the truth of either entails the falsity of the other, so they cannot both be true or both be false. Thus, the conditions of contradiction are present in them.

The most they can say by way of distinction is that, when we say, “He is either sighted or not sighted,” this is affirmation and negation, whereas, when we say, “He is either sighted or blind,” this is possession and privation. This is merely a verbal dispute; otherwise, the meaning is the same in both cases.

It is therefore clear that this is a kind of opposition between negation and affirmation. This invalidates their assertion, in defining that opposition, that neither alternative can change into the other. For such a change is possible here, just as it is possible when the expression “blindness” is used.

(The third argument is to say: An exhaustive division is formulated as follows: two opposites either

(ك)
differ by negation and affirmation, or do not differ in this way, but are both affirmative or both negative. In the first case, they are contradictories.

In the second case, either the subject can be devoid of both, or it cannot. In the former case, they are contraries, such as blackness and whiteness. In the latter, they have the force of contradictories, even if both are positive attributes, such as necessity and contingency, temporal origination and eternity, subsistence in oneself and subsistence in another, separation and adjacency, and the like. It is well known that life and death, deafness, muteness, and hearing do not allow a subject devoid of the opposing attributes to be described by a third, intermediate attribute, as redness lies between blackness and whiteness. Thus, the subject cannot be devoid of both opposites: if one is absent, the other must be present.

(The fourth argument: A subject incapable of bearing attributes such as life, knowledge, power, and speech is more deficient than one capable of bearing them but lacking them. Hence, a stone and the like are more deficient than a living being who is blind. Accordingly, if the Creator is exalted above lacking these attributes while being capable of bearing them, then He must, with even greater reason, be exalted above being incapable of bearing them. For, on the assumption that He can bear them, both opposites cannot be absent; and since His possessing attributes of deficiency is impossible, He must possess attributes of perfection. But on the assumption that He cannot bear them,

He can possess neither attributes of perfection nor attributes of deficiency, and this is even more impossible. Thus, it is established that His possessing these attributes is possible and, indeed, necessary for Him—which is what was to be demonstrated. This is an excellent argument.

(The fifth argument:
It may be said: You have confined the opposition of privation and possession to that which can possess a positive attribute. If by possibility you mean actual possibility—that the attribute is known to exist in external reality—then this is invalid for two reasons:

First, it entails that inanimate things cannot be described as neither living nor dead, neither speaking nor silent—which is your position, although this is merely a terminological convention—and that you should not describe these inanimate things as dead or silent. Yet the Quran does describe them in this way. Allah Most High says:

“Those they invoke besides Allah create nothing, while they themselves are created.”

“They are dead, not alive, and they do not know when they will be resurrected.”

This concerns “idols,” which are inanimate things, yet they are described as dead. The Arabs divide the earth into hayawān (the living) and mawatān (the lifeless). The lexicographers say: “Mawatān, with the middle consonant vowelled, is the opposite of hayawān. It is said: ‘Buy the lifeless, and do not buy the living’—that is, buy land and houses, but do not buy slaves and beasts.”

They also say: “Mawāt is that which has no soul.” If it is said, “This is called mawāt only with reference to his expression ‘for life,’ meaning the revival of land,” the reply is: This implies that life is broader than the life of animate beings, and that an inanimate thing may be described as living when it is capable of cultivation and development. Muteness is the opposite of speech, and the Arabs say

---

## Page 978

(م)
“Mute milk,” meaning thickened milk that makes no sound in its vessel; “a mute cloud,” one without thunder or lightning; and “a mute landmark,” when no echo from it is heard in the mountain. They also say, “a mute military formation.” Abū Ubayda said: “It is one rendered silent by the abundance of armour, making no clattering sound.”

An even stronger example is silence and refraining from speech: these describe someone capable of speaking who refrains from doing so, unlike muteness, which is an inability to speak. Nevertheless, the Arabs say, “He possesses neither anything silent nor anything speaking.” Here, “silent” means gold and silver, while “speaking” means camels and sheep. “Silent” milk is thickened milk, and “silent armour” is armour that makes no audible sound.

They also describe an animal as “speechless” and “mute” because it does not speak and cannot ordinarily do so. Hence the Prophet’s saying, may Allah bless him and grant him peace:

“Injury caused by a speechless animal entails no compensation.”

The same applies to expressions denoting blindness. The Arabs say, “‘Amā al-mawj,” with the present tense ya‘mī and the verbal noun ‘amyan, when waves cast forth debris and foam. “The two blind ones” are a torrent and a raging camel. They also say that a matter “became blind to him” when it became unclear. Hence Allah Most High says:

“But the information will be obscured to them that Day.”

Of some of these examples, it may be said that they involve the absence of an attribute which the subject is capable of possessing, such as sound. Others, however, involve attributes which the subject cannot possess, as in the death of idols.

Sixth argument: It may be said: Grant that knowledge of possibility in external reality is necessary. The possibility of something possessing an attribute may be known through considerations pertaining to it, through the attribute’s presence in something comparable to it, or through its presence in something less entitled to that attribute than it is. It is known that life, knowledge, power, hearing, sight, and speech are established attributes of created beings and are possible for them. Their possibility for the Creator, Most High, is therefore all the more fitting and appropriate, for they are attributes of perfection, and He can possess attributes. Since they are possible for Him, were He not to possess them, He would possess their opposites.

Seventh argument: It may be said: The mere absence of these attributes is intrinsically an imperfection, whether it is called blindness, deafness, and muteness or not. This is known self-evidently. If we suppose two existing beings, one of which hears, sees, and speaks, while the other does not, the first is more perfect than the second. For this reason, Allah, glorified be He, censured those who worshipped things lacking these attributes. Thus the Most High said concerning

“Why do you worship what neither hears nor sees, nor benefits you in any way?”

“Do they hear you when you call, or benefit you or harm you? They said, ‘Rather, we found our fathers doing the same.’ He said, ‘Have you considered what you have been worshipping—you and your forefathers? They are enemies to me, except the Lord of all worlds.’”

“Did they not see that it neither spoke to them nor guided them to any way? They took it for worship and were wrongdoers.”

“Allah sets forth a comparison between two men: one is mute, unable to do anything, and a burden to his master. Wherever he directs him, he brings no good. Is he equal to one who commands justice and follows a straight path?”

Thus He contrasted the mute, powerless man with the one who commands justice and follows a straight path.
